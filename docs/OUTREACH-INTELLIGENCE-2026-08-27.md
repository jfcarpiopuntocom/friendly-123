## Section 1: Voice of the Vendor

### What vendors complain about
Across Reddit and public discussions, the dominant pain is that paper notebooks, spreadsheets, and ad hoc systems collapse once volume grows. The same pattern shows up in reselling, Etsy, craft fair vending, consignment, and flea market work: people can handle a few items or a small booth, then the overhead starts eating time.

Representative quotes and paraphrases from real discussions:
- "I used spreadsheets for a decade... I'm 'math braindead' and hate the manual labor of maintaining complex formulas."
- "At 3–5 items a day it's manageable, but it becomes really challenging at 15 items a day."
- "I abandoned my spreadsheet entirely on the first day I hit 50 sales."
- "The time spent on manual data entry equals the time spent shipping items."
- "Once you're juggling vendor splits, taxes, and stock counts, the notebook stops being enough."

The recurring complaint is not just "I need software." It is more specific:
- manual entry takes too long
- formulas break
- tracking multiple locations or booths gets messy
- commissions and consignment splits are hard to calculate
- inventory gets duplicated or lost
- it is hard to know what sold, what is reserved, and what needs restocking
- existing software feels built for accountants, not makers or vendors

### What software they use and what they hate about it
Common tools mentioned include:
- Excel / Google Sheets: cheap and flexible, but tedious, fragile, and hard to maintain at scale
- Square: used as POS, but sellers still often export into spreadsheets for real inventory and commission tracking
- Shopify / Etsy tools: useful for online order flows, but often not aligned with in-person booth, fair, and consignment realities
- QuickBooks / Zoho Books / GoDaddy Bookkeeping: powerful but too heavy or expensive for many small operators
- Airtable / Notion: often adopted as a bridge, but still require custom setup and discipline
- ConsignPro / SimpleConsign / Ricochet: more relevant for consignment stores, but can feel specialized or pricey
- Paper notebooks: loved for simplicity, but fail when there are multiple booths, vendors, stock colors, and sales channels

What they hate most is not the category of tool; it is the friction:
- too many tabs
- too many formulas
- too many steps to record a sale
- too much time reconciling stock later
- poor fit with mobile use in a market booth
- no easy way to capture quick notes about customers, commissions, or reserved items

### The language they use
The vendor language is blunt and operational. They say things like:
- "manageable" vs "really challenging"
- "manual labor"
- "math braindead"
- "data entry"
- "inventory headaches"
- "keeping track"
- "sales are too messy"
- "I need something simple"
- "I can't keep up"
- "too much admin"
- "it becomes a full-time job"

That language matters because it signals that the winning message is not "AI-powered business operations." It is closer to:
- "less manual data entry"
- "keep track without spreadsheets"
- "know what sold in seconds"
- "handle commissions without the math"
- "works on your phone at the booth"

### What regulars, commissions, and consignment mean in practice
In these communities:
- **Regulars** usually means repeat customers who come back to the booth/shop and expect recognition, preference, or a saved history.
- **Commissions** usually means custom-made work ordered by a customer, often with deposit, status, special instructions, and pickup/delivery tracking.
- **Consignment** usually means inventory owned by another seller or maker, sold through a shop/booth, with revenue split by percentage and payout timing.

Practically, this means a good notebook has to track:
- item owner
- item status
- price
- commission terms
- payout due
- customer identity
- reserved or custom orders
- multi-location movement
- sold vs unsold vs returned

### Themes around going digital
The emotional picture is mixed:

**Excitement:**
- relief from mental overload
- faster sales tracking
- easier tax prep
- fewer missed items
- more professional-looking operations

**Fear / resistance:**
- "I'm not techy"
- fear of complexity
- fear of subscriptions
- fear of losing records if the app or internet fails
- fear of having to re-enter everything
- fear that a digital tool will slow them down at the booth

**Barriers to adoption:**
- poor cell service at fairs or markets
- low tolerance for setup
- reluctance to pay monthly fees
- need for offline use
- need for simple onboarding
- need to work on a phone browser without installing anything

### Why friendly-123 fits the gap
The strongest product-market language for this audience is:
- offline first
- no app install
- no subscription for core features
- phone-friendly at the booth
- simple color-coded stock status
- commissions and client notes in one place
- multi-location support for pop-ups, shelves, booths, and consignments

The seller is not buying "software." They are buying relief from chaos.
